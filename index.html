Tracking every rupee of cash on delivery, from a rider's pocket to the bank

KNOT delivers fashion in 60 minutes from dark stores in Mumbai, and a large share of customers pay cash at the door. I diagnosed why cash kept going missing, redesigned how it is settled, and specified the system that now tracks it in production.

How it used to work

A warehouse team member would call a rider, pull the day's data, and ask for the money one order at a time. Marking a single order as settled took several clicks across different pages. At 100 to 150 orders a day that was tedious but manageable. At around 1,000 orders a day, with 55% paid in cash, that meant roughly 550 separate settlements every day across 200 riders, and it fell apart.

Cash sat with riders for days. Some spent it on household needs and asked for it to be cut from their salary instead, which created a second thing to track: who owed what through deductions. Mismatches only surfaced at the fortnightly rider payout, and sometimes a month later.

The reframe

The unit of settlement was wrong. An order is how the customer pays, but a rider is the person who physically carries the money. So riders now pack the day's cash into one bundle, the manager counts it and accepts it in one action, and each order stays attached underneath for tracing. On top of that sits a custody ledger: money is always in exactly one state, and it only moves when the next person verifies it.

The full process

Cash leaves the customer's hand and passes through three people before it reaches the bank. Each of those people must accept the money before they become responsible for it, and Finance closes the loop by checking the bank statement. A QR option lets a rider pay the cash straight into the company account, skipping the physical chain.

Eight states, and nothing in between

Six custody states follow the money. Two end states cover cash that is written off and deliveries that never really happened. The backend rejects any move that isn't on this diagram, so nobody can deposit cash a rider still holds, and nobody can quietly edit an amount.

Key design decisions

  1. An append-only record for every movement

    Each change stores who did it, their role, the reason, the time, and the before and after state. The database blocks edits and deletes, which is what made Finance willing to trust the numbers.

  2. Nobody certifies their own handover

    The area manager counts the rider's cash, the store manager counts the area manager's, and Finance checks the deposit against the bank. Deposits carry photo proof.

  3. Track pieces of money, not whole orders

    Riders sometimes hand over part of an order's cash and bring the rest later. Freezing the whole order would stall money already in hand, so the system splits it.

    ₹800Handed over now, moves on to the bank today
    ₹200Stays with the rider as its own piece, tracked until collected

  4. A QR path around the physical chain

    A manager generates a payment QR for one order or a whole bundle. Once the payment gateway confirms, that cash goes straight to the bank state. The cash is reserved before the gateway is called, and stale reservations clear themselves, so a failed request never strands money.

  5. Access by capability, fenced by store

    Permissions are granted one at a time, so a store lead can hold both manager roles. People only see their own stores, and identity always comes from the signed-in session, never from what the app sends.

When the clean flow breaks

Most of the design effort went into exceptions, because that's where money goes missing. Every deviation is recorded as an exception. There's no side ledger for adjustments.

My part

I spotted that order-by-order settlement would not survive our growth and reframed the settlement unit around the rider. I wrote the PRD, functional spec and technical spec, including the state model, exception handling and approval rules. I worked through the edge cases with Ops and store teams, ran UAT, and I own the system in production. Engineering built it. After launch I wrote an as-built PRD that documents every state, role, screen, exception and dashboard number, so Ops and Finance work from one reference that matches the live system.

What's still open

Two gaps remain. Because shortfalls now show up against a bundle, finding the exact order still relies on the order-level detail underneath. And when riders pick up returned items but record "nothing returned", the expected cash is wrong before it ever enters this system, so that fix belongs in the rider app's returns flow.

What I took away

The hard part was deciding what the system should refuse. Every shortcut that looked helpful, like editing a wrong amount or depositing cash still out with a rider, broke the one promise Finance cared about. Writing the rules down as a state machine first made those calls easy to defend.
